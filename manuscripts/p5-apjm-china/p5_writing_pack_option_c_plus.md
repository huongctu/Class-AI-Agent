## §2. Theory and Hypotheses (terminology-harmonized)

### 2.1 Internationalization and firm performance

The relationship between firm internationalization and firm performance is unlikely to be linear for manufacturing SMEs. At lower to moderate levels, internationalization may improve performance by enlarging market reach, spreading fixed costs, increasing scale economies, and allowing firms to exploit existing production capabilities more fully across foreign markets. For smaller firms in particular, participation in foreign markets may also stimulate learning, sharpen quality discipline, and diversify revenue sources beyond the domestic market.

However, the advantages of internationalization are not unlimited. As firms become more deeply internationalized, they face greater coordination demands, increased exposure to foreign-market volatility, more complex logistics and payment arrangements, and higher managerial burdens associated with serving external markets on a sustained basis. These costs are especially consequential for manufacturing SMEs because they typically operate with narrower financial slack, more limited organizational depth, and less capacity to absorb shocks than larger multinational firms.

In the present study, firm internationalization is operationalized as **export intensity**, captured by the share of foreign sales in total sales. Under this operationalization, internationalization should improve firm performance up to a point, but performance should deteriorate once export intensity becomes too dominant relative to the firm's operational and financial capacity. The relevant expectation is therefore not a monotonic positive effect, but an inverted U-shaped association in which the performance gains of internationalization are bounded.

> **Hypothesis 1 (H1).** Firm internationalization, measured as export intensity, has an inverted U-shaped relationship with firm performance among Chinese manufacturing SMEs.

### 2.2 Stability of the internationalization threshold

Although inverted U-shaped relationships are common in the internationalization literature, a more demanding question is whether the estimated turning point is stable over time. A nonlinear association observed in a single sample may reflect period-specific conditions, temporary shocks, or sample composition effects rather than an enduring structural trade-off. For that reason, identifying an inverted U-shape is only the first step; a stronger contribution requires showing that the implied threshold remains substantively similar across distinct survey waves.

The argument for threshold stability rests on the idea that the core trade-off faced by Chinese manufacturing SMEs is structural. Internationalization, observed here through export intensity, generates learning and market-access benefits at lower to moderate levels, but beyond a bounded range it increasingly strains firm resources, managerial attention, and operating discipline. Even though external market conditions may change between 2012 and 2024, the underlying tension between scale-related gains and overextension costs should remain sufficiently persistent to produce a broadly stable optimal range rather than a radically shifting one.

> **Hypothesis 2 (H2).** The turning point in the inverted U-shaped relationship between internationalization, measured as export intensity, and firm performance is substantively stable across the 2012 and 2024 China survey waves.

### 2.3 Working-capital pressure and the post-threshold downturn

A central interpretation of the downturn segment is that very high levels of internationalization, expressed here through export intensity, can intensify working-capital pressure. Manufacturing exporters frequently face a timing gap between production outlays and revenue realization, particularly when sales are made on credit, payment terms are delayed, shipping cycles are extended, or trade-finance access is imperfect. As export intensity rises, this gap can become more consequential because a larger share of firm activity becomes tied to transactions with longer or less predictable cash-conversion dynamics.

This argument does not deny that coordination costs, market complexity, and organizational burdens also matter. Rather, it specifies a more concrete economic mechanism through which the negative segment of the curve may emerge. Firms with weaker liquidity access, heavier receivables exposure, or greater dependence on internally financed working capital should be less able to sustain very high export intensity without performance erosion.

The implication is not that working-capital conditions create the entire nonlinear relationship from the outset. Instead, they should matter most in shaping how sharply performance deteriorates once firms move beyond the optimal internationalization range.

> **Hypothesis 3 (H3).** Adverse working-capital conditions strengthen the negative post-threshold segment of the inverted U-shaped relationship between internationalization, measured as export intensity, and firm performance among Chinese manufacturing SMEs.

### 2.4 Digital adoption as a secondary performance condition

The role of digital adoption in P5 is theoretically relevant but secondary to the threshold logic. Firms with stronger digital positioning may benefit from better information processing, smoother internal coordination, improved communication with customers and suppliers, and more efficient monitoring of operations. These advantages can improve general firm efficiency and may therefore be associated with higher average performance levels.

At the same time, the present paper does not treat digital adoption as the principal explanation for the nonlinear threshold itself. This is partly an architectural choice, because P5 is centered on threshold stability and working-capital interpretation, and partly a measurement choice, because the available WBES indicators are better suited to capturing lower-tier digital adoption than a fully elaborated dynamic digital capability.

> **Hypothesis 4 (H4).** Higher digital adoption is positively associated with firm performance among Chinese manufacturing SMEs.

## §3. Methods β block

### Long version (3 paragraphs, ~400 words)

To extend the threshold analysis without altering the manuscript's core identity, supplementary models were estimated to examine whether the negative segment of the export intensity–performance curve is conditioned by firm-level working-capital circumstances. The motivation for this extension follows the paper's central interpretation that excessive export intensity may lengthen cash-conversion cycles, increase receivables exposure, and intensify liquidity strain for manufacturing SMEs operating in imperfect credit environments. Because the available WBES indicators do not provide a single direct measure of the working-capital trap, the analysis relies on multiple firm-level proxies capturing liquidity access, trade-credit exposure, and working-capital financing structure.

The retained working-capital proxies were organized into three blocks. First, liquidity-access measures capture whether the firm possesses formal short-term funding buffers, including overdraft facilities and credit-line or loan access. Second, trade-credit exposure measures capture the degree to which firm cash recovery may be delayed through sales conducted on credit and, in robustness tests, purchases financed on credit. Third, financing-structure measures capture how current operations are funded, especially the extent to which working capital depends on internal funds versus external financial support. These variables are not treated as reflective indicators of a latent psychometric construct, but as observable firm-level conditions that jointly approximate different aspects of working-capital stress.

The supplementary mechanism-oriented models retain the baseline quadratic specification of the paper and then introduce working-capital variables and interaction terms. The focal parameter is the interaction between the squared export-intensity term and each working-capital measure, because the theoretical argument concerns the steepness of the post-threshold downturn rather than the initial upward phase alone. A negative interaction is expected when the working-capital measure is coded as stress or constraint, whereas a positive interaction is expected when the measure is coded as liquidity access or financing support. To preserve comparability with the main specification, all supplementary models maintain the original control structure and are estimated for the pooled sample as well as separately for the 2012 and 2024 waves.

### Digital retention paragraph

Digital variables are retained in the supplementary models, but under Option C+ they are treated as secondary explanatory architecture rather than as the manuscript's central contribution. More specifically, the digital term is used primarily to assess whether firms with stronger digital positioning operate at a higher overall performance level and whether the working-capital interpretation remains substantively intact after accounting for digital heterogeneity. Consistent with the measurement limits of the WBES, the digital construct is described conservatively as digital adoption or lower-tier digitalization rather than as a fully developed dynamic digital capability. For this reason, digital interaction terms are treated as diagnostic or robustness specifications rather than as identity-defining tests.

### Closing sentence (locks reading order)

Accordingly, the supplementary analyses are interpreted hierarchically. Evidence from the threshold models remains primary, evidence from the working-capital interactions is used to evaluate the paper's proposed economic interpretation, and evidence from digital terms is used mainly to assess performance level shifts and robustness rather than to redefine the manuscript's theoretical center.

### Short version (1 paragraph if word count is tight)

To probe the paper's working-capital interpretation more directly, supplementary regressions introduce firm-level proxies for liquidity access, trade-credit exposure, and working-capital financing structure into the baseline quadratic export-intensity model. The focal test is the interaction between each working-capital measure and the squared export-intensity term, as the theory concerns whether post-threshold performance deterioration becomes steeper under tighter liquidity conditions. Digital variables are retained as secondary controls and direct-effect terms, with interaction-based digital specifications treated as robustness checks only; this reflects the manuscript's Option C+ architecture, under which threshold stability remains the core contribution.

### 4.X — supported

The supplementary results indicate that the interaction between the squared export-intensity term and the focal working-capital measure is directionally consistent with the proposed trap logic and statistically meaningful in the pooled specification. Substantively, this pattern suggests that the post-threshold decline becomes steeper among firms facing greater liquidity pressure, which is consistent with the interpretation that excessive export intensity intensifies working-capital strain in Chinese manufacturing SMEs.

At the same time, the retained digital variable is positively associated with firm performance at the level term, indicating that firms with stronger digital positioning tend to perform better overall. However, the digital interaction terms do not display a comparably stable effect on the curvature of the export intensity–performance relationship across specifications. Taken together, these findings preserve the paper's threshold-stability core while providing more direct support for the working-capital interpretation of the downturn.

These supplementary results do not alter the manuscript's central claim, but they sharpen its economic interpretation. ### 4.X — limited

The supplementary working-capital tests yield mixed but suggestive evidence. Several coefficients move in the theoretically expected direction, particularly in the pooled specification, yet the pattern is not fully uniform across alternative proxy constructions or across the 2012 and 2024 waves. Accordingly, the results are consistent with a working-capital contingency, but they do not justify a stronger claim that the mechanism is directly and stably identified.

The digital term, by contrast, remains more regular at the level of direct association than at the level of interaction. Firms with stronger digital positioning tend to exhibit higher performance levels overall, but the digital interaction terms provide limited evidence that digitalization systematically reshapes the curvature of the export intensity–performance relationship. This pattern is broadly consistent with the interpretation that digitalization raises operational efficiency while leaving the threshold structure largely intact. The paper therefore remains best understood as a threshold-stability study with a theoretically grounded but only partially supported working-capital interpretation.

These results matter less for redefining the paper than for clarifying the limits of its explanatory reach. The next section considers what this combination of stable threshold evidence and only partial mechanism support implies for theory, practice, and future data collection.

### 4.X — null

The direct working-capital tests do not yield sufficiently robust support across alternative proxy definitions and wave-specific models. Although some coefficients move in theoretically plausible directions, the pattern is not stable enough to support a strong claim that the post-threshold downturn has been directly identified as a working-capital mechanism in the present data. This outcome leaves the threshold-stability result unchanged while limiting the strength of direct mechanism inference.

The retained digital variable remains positively associated with firm performance at the level term, but its interaction terms again show little evidence of systematically altering the nonlinear structure of the curve. In substantive terms, digitalization appears to improve the general performance level of firms without eliminating the structural export-intensity threshold identified in the main models. For that reason, the manuscript retains working-capital trap logic as a theoretically grounded interpretation rather than as a directly established empirical mechanism.

Rather than weakening the paper, this result sharpens its scope. The contribution remains the identification of a stable export-intensity threshold for Chinese manufacturing SMEs, while the discussion considers how future research could test liquidity channels more directly using richer financial microdata.

## §5. Discussion × 3 scenarios

### 5.1 — supported

#### Threshold stability and the working-capital interpretation

The findings position P5 most clearly as a threshold-stability study. The central result is not merely that export intensity is associated with performance in a nonlinear way, but that the estimated turning point remains substantively stable across the 2012 and 2024 China waves, suggesting that the underlying trade-off is structural rather than transitory. This stability matters because it shifts attention away from a simple "more exporting is always better" logic and toward the managerial problem of calibrating export intensity within a bounded efficiency range.

The supplementary analyses strengthen the paper's proposed economic interpretation of this threshold. Specifically, the evidence indicates that the post-threshold decline is steeper under more adverse working-capital conditions, which is consistent with the argument that liquidity pressure helps explain why additional export expansion can become counterproductive for manufacturing SMEs. This does not mean that all coordination or organizational complexity arguments are irrelevant; rather, in the Chinese SME setting, the financial side of internationalization deserves more explicit attention than is often granted in generic nonlinear internationalization models.

The role of digitalization in this setting appears more limited but still meaningful. The retained digital variable is more consistent as a positive level shifter than as a curvature modifier, which suggests that digital adoption may improve efficiency, information flow, or operating discipline without fundamentally removing the liquidity pressures associated with very high export intensity.

#### Implications

For theory, the results suggest that nonlinear internationalization should be interpreted not only through coordination-cost logic but also through financing frictions that become binding beyond a threshold. For practice, the findings imply that SME managers should treat export expansion as a calibrated intensity decision rather than an unconstrained growth objective. For methodology, the paper shows the value of keeping mechanism-oriented tests subordinate to a clearly identified core contribution.

#### Limitations (continues into §6)

Although the supplementary working-capital evidence is supportive, the available WBES proxies still do not capture the liquidity channel as directly as panel data on receivables duration, cash-conversion cycles, or trade-finance pricing would allow.

### 5.2 — limited

#### Threshold stability and bounded mechanism evidence

The strongest contribution of P5 remains the identification of a stable export-intensity threshold for Chinese manufacturing SMEs. The recurring turning point across the 2012 and 2024 waves indicates that the relevant trade-off is not simply episodic noise, but a durable structural feature of the export–performance relationship in this setting.

The supplementary working-capital analyses provide partial support for the paper's preferred interpretation of this pattern. Several estimates are directionally consistent with the proposed liquidity-pressure logic, yet the evidence does not converge strongly enough across waves and operationalizations to justify a stronger claim of direct mechanism identification. This mixed result is still informative; it suggests that working-capital strain is plausibly part of the explanation for the downturn, but not one that can be treated as decisively established with the available proxies.

Digitalization again appears more regular as a level effect than as a structural moderator of curvature. Such a result fits comfortably with Option C+ because it retains digital heterogeneity in the analysis without allowing it to displace the central threshold contribution.

### 5.3 — null

#### Threshold stability as the principal contribution

The discussion is clearest when the paper is read first and foremost as a threshold-stability study. The main result is that export intensity exhibits a stable inverted U-shaped association with firm performance across the 2012 and 2024 China waves, with the turning point remaining within a substantively similar range. This alone is a meaningful contribution because it identifies a bounded optimal export-intensity zone for Chinese manufacturing SMEs rather than reducing the internationalization question to a linear growth logic.

The supplementary working-capital tests do not provide sufficiently robust support to elevate the paper's preferred interpretation into a directly established empirical mechanism. While some coefficients move in plausible directions, the broader pattern is too inconsistent across proxies and waves to sustain a stronger inference. That limitation should not be viewed as a failure of the paper's central contribution. Instead, it clarifies the proper scope of the manuscript: the evidence strongly supports threshold stability, while the working-capital trap remains a theoretically grounded and managerially intuitive interpretation that warrants more direct future testing.

The digital results point in a similar direction of bounded interpretation. Digitalization appears to raise the general level of firm performance, but it does not provide stable evidence of altering the threshold structure of the export intensity–performance curve.

### Universal Discussion closing sentence (use in all scenarios)

Overall, the evidence is strongest for a stable export-intensity threshold, whereas the working-capital and digital analyses refine the interpretation of that threshold without displacing it as the paper's central contribution.

## §6. Conclusion × 3 scenarios

### 6 — supported

This study shows that the relationship between export intensity and firm performance among Chinese manufacturing SMEs is best understood as a threshold phenomenon rather than a linear growth pattern. The evidence indicates a stable inverted U-shaped association across the 2012 and 2024 waves, with performance gains rising up to a bounded export-intensity range before declining beyond it. The central contribution of the paper, therefore, lies in identifying a stable optimal export-intensity zone rather than in simply reaffirming that nonlinear internationalization exists.

The supplementary analyses also provide more direct support for the paper's working-capital interpretation of the downturn. Firms facing tighter liquidity conditions appear to experience a steeper post-threshold decline, which is consistent with the argument that export expansion eventually collides with financing frictions in the SME context. Digitalization appears to operate more as a positive performance level shifter than as a force that systematically restructures the threshold itself. Taken together, these results position P5 as a threshold-stability study with strengthened evidence for a working-capital contingency.

Future research can extend this contribution by testing the same threshold logic with more direct measures of receivables duration, cash-conversion cycles, and trade-finance access.

### 6 — limited

This study identifies a stable threshold structure in the export intensity–performance relationship among Chinese manufacturing SMEs. Across the 2012 and 2024 waves, the results point to a recurring inverted U-shaped pattern in which export expansion is beneficial up to a bounded range but becomes increasingly difficult to sustain beyond that point. The main contribution of the paper is therefore the documentation of threshold stability, not merely the reporting of nonlinear association.

The supplementary analyses offer partial but not fully convergent support for the paper's working-capital interpretation. Some evidence is consistent with the view that liquidity pressure contributes to the post-threshold downturn, yet the available proxy-based tests do not warrant a stronger claim of stable direct mechanism identification. Digitalization again appears more regular as a positive level effect than as a curvature-changing force. Accordingly, P5 is best read as a threshold-stability paper with bounded empirical support for a working-capital interpretation.

Future research should revisit this mechanism using richer financial microdata and longitudinal designs that observe liquidity stress more directly.

### 6 — null

This study demonstrates that export intensity and firm performance are linked through a stable threshold structure among Chinese manufacturing SMEs. The evidence from the 2012 and 2024 waves consistently supports an inverted U-shaped association, indicating that export expansion is beneficial only up to a bounded range and that overextension beyond that range is associated with declining performance. That threshold-stability finding is the principal contribution of the paper and stands independently of any stronger mechanism claim.

The supplementary working-capital tests do not provide sufficiently robust support to convert the paper's preferred interpretation into a directly established empirical mechanism. For that reason, the working-capital trap remains best understood as a theoretically grounded explanation of the downturn rather than as a channel definitively identified in the current data. Digitalization, meanwhile, appears to raise performance levels more than it alters the threshold structure itself, reinforcing its secondary role within the present manuscript.

Future research should test the liquidity interpretation with more direct measures of receivables management, trade-finance access, and cash-cycle pressure.

### Universal Conclusion last sentence (any scenario)

Overall, the study contributes most clearly by showing that the export–performance trade-off for Chinese manufacturing SMEs is bounded, stable, and strategically consequential, even when the precise financial channel behind that threshold remains stronger in some specifications than in others.
