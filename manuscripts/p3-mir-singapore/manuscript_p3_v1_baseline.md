**Technological Capability, Digital Adoption, and the Internationalization--Performance Relationship in Singapore**

**Abstract**

This study examines whether technological capability and digital adoption play distinct roles in shaping the internationalization--performance relationship in a digital-frontier economy. Using World Bank Enterprise Survey microdata for Singapore 2023 (N = 623), the analysis develops a two-construct measurement architecture that separates a Technological Capability Index (TCI), anchored in the Lall (1992) capability tradition, from a Digital Adoption Index (DAI), anchored in the digital strategy and digitalization literature (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Verhoef et al., 2021). TCI is positively associated with labour productivity and operates as a level-shift effect on the productivity intercept rather than as a curvature-shaping moderator. The baseline internationalization--performance pattern is best characterized as primarily monotonic with mild quadratic curvature within the observed export-intensity range; given the thin right tail of the FSTS distribution, the inferred turning point should be read as suggestive rather than as formally identified evidence of a conventional inverted-U. DAI shows conditional relevance: its productivity association becomes more positive at higher levels of export intensity, although the evidence is concentrated in the high-export tail rather than distributed uniformly across the sample. These findings suggest that, in a digitally mature institutional environment, digital adoption may matter less as a universal firm-level productivity premium and more as a scale-enabling complement for firms facing more demanding cross-border coordination. The study contributes sharper construct validity and an initial boundary-condition argument for internationalization research in digital-frontier settings.

**Keywords: internationalization--performance; digital adoption; technological capability; boundary conditions; World Bank Enterprise Survey**

**1 Introduction**

**1.1 Background and motivation**

Singapore is among the most digitally advanced economies in Asia: by 2023, more than 90% of Singaporean firms maintained a digital presence and over half processed customer payments digitally (World Bank, 2024). Yet whether firm-level digital adoption translates into productivity returns at the export margin remains unsettled, particularly when firms scale into international markets. The internationalization--performance (I--P) literature has produced a family of nonlinear specifications --- including inverted-U findings (Hitt et al., 1997) and three-stage S-curve formulations (Contractor et al., 2003; Lu & Beamish, 2004) --- that share a common claim that the marginal productivity returns to internationalization are not constant across the FSTS distribution; subsequent meta-analytic and review work has consolidated the inverted-U as the modal pattern across this literature (Marano et al., 2016). Both strands were developed largely on samples from pre-digital or transitional-digital economies and may not capture how the I--P curvature behaves when firms operate in a digitally mature institutional environment.

In a digitally mature economy, two distinct firm-level capabilities operate simultaneously. Technological capability --- firm-internal capability depth in R&D, innovation, and quality --- follows the Lall (1992) tradition and the Cohen and Levinthal (1990) absorptive-capacity logic. Digital adoption --- firm participation in cross-border digital infrastructure and digital transactions --- follows the digital strategy tradition (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Verhoef et al., 2021). Whether these two capabilities play distinct or overlapping roles in shaping firm performance at different stages of internationalization is, ex ante, an empirical question.

**1.2 Research gap**

Three gaps in the literature motivate this study. First, the dominant measurement approach has tended to conflate digital adoption with broader technological capability, obscuring whether observed empirical patterns derive from firm-internal capability depth or firm--environment digital integration (Verhoef et al., 2021). Second, recent work on digital internationalization has often taken the form of theoretical frameworks (Banalieva & Dhanaraj, 2019; Stallkamp & Schotter, 2021) or has focused on multinational subsamples, leaving the within-economy firm-level question --- how digital adoption interacts with export intensity in a single, digitally mature institutional environment --- under-examined. Third, the I--P curvature literature has documented inverted-U patterns extensively but has under-theorized how the underlying coordination-cost mechanism behaves in environments where cross-border digital infrastructure is mature (Marano et al., 2016).

**1.3 Contribution**

We make three contributions. First, we sharpen construct validity in digital-internationalization research by distinguishing technological capability from digital adoption rather than combining them into a single umbrella construct. This distinction matters because the two constructs capture analytically different sources of firm heterogeneity: technological capability reflects internal capability depth, whereas digital adoption reflects firms\' uptake of digitally enabled interfaces and transaction mechanisms.

Second, we provide a firm-level test of the internationalization--performance relationship using WBES 2023 microdata and show that the conventional inverted-U is not formally supported within the observed export-intensity range. Rather than overturning the established I--P literature, this finding helps refine its boundary conditions by suggesting that, in a digitally mature institutional environment, the right-side decline of the curve may be attenuated or shifted toward sparsely populated levels of export intensity.

Third, we show that digital adoption in a digital-frontier economy is better understood as a scale-contingent complement than as a uniform firm-level productivity premium. Its productivity relevance becomes more visible at higher levels of export intensity, where digital systems can be leveraged across a broader set of cross-border transactions and coordination demands. This contributes an initial boundary-condition argument for internationalization research in digitally mature settings and clarifies why digital adoption should be treated separately from technological capability in firm-level models.

**2 Theory and Hypotheses**

**2.1 The internationalization--performance relationship**

The I--P literature has produced extensive evidence of nonlinear relationships between the degree of internationalization and firm performance. Hitt et al. (1997) introduced the inverted-U logic: initial internationalization yields scale and learning benefits, but at high export intensities firms encounter cognitive and coordination ceilings that erode the returns to further expansion. Contractor et al. (2003) extended this logic to a three-stage S-curve, and Lu and Beamish (2004) refined the inverted-U with attention to firm size and age effects. The modal pattern across this literature is an inverted-U with a turning point in the 30%--60% foreign-sales-to-total-sales (FSTS) range (Marano et al., 2016).

A boundary condition implicit in this literature, but rarely articulated, is that the inverted-U requires a coordination-cost mechanism that scales with internationalization intensity. If a firm operates in an institutional environment where coordination costs have already been substantially attenuated --- for example, by mature cross-border digital infrastructure --- the right-side decline of the inverted-U may be flattened or shifted to levels of internationalization that are sparsely represented in the observed FSTS distribution. Singapore is a natural setting in which to examine how these boundary conditions operate at the firm level.

**2.2 Two distinct constructs: TCI and DAI**

A recurring problem in the digital international business literature is the tendency to collapse technologically heterogeneous firm attributes into a single umbrella construct, thereby blurring the distinction between internal capability depth and digitally enabled market participation (Verhoef et al., 2021). This study departs from that practice by distinguishing a Technological Capability Index (TCI), anchored in the Lall--Cohen-Levinthal capability tradition (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Lall, 1992), from a Digital Adoption Index (DAI), anchored in the Bharadwaj--Verhoef digitalization tradition (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Verhoef et al., 2021).

The distinction is theoretically necessary because the two constructs refer to different domains of firm advantage. TCI captures internal capability stocks related to innovation, learning, and technology absorption, drawing on the absorptive-capacity logic of Cohen and Levinthal (1990) and the technological-capability tradition of Lall (1992). DAI, by contrast, captures the firm\'s uptake of digital interfaces and transaction-enabling mechanisms (Bharadwaj et al., 2013), reflecting a more environmentally contingent form of firm advantage that depends on the maturity of the surrounding digital ecosystem (Verhoef et al., 2021).

Equally important, the label \"digital adoption\" is more defensible than \"digital capability\" in the present empirical setting because the WBES indicators available here map primarily onto basic digital presence and digital transaction usage rather than deeper digitally integrated organizational processes. In the Verhoef et al. (2021) hierarchy, website presence (c22b) corresponds most closely to Tier 1 digitization, while electronic-payment intensity on the customer and supplier sides (k33, k38) corresponds to Tier 2 digitalization. Our DAI composite therefore captures the foundational digital layer upon which higher-order digital capabilities may be built, but it cannot directly observe Tier 3 process integration (e.g., ERP, CRM, digitally integrated supply chains) or Tier 4 digital dynamic capability (e.g., AI deployment, platform orchestration, reconfiguration capability). Given this measurement boundary, retaining a broad \"digital capability\" label would overstate what the underlying indicators can legitimately support, whereas the DAI label improves construct validity by aligning the variable name with the actual content domain of the observed measures. Methodologically, the two constructs satisfy the Coltman et al. (2008) four-criterion test for formative (as opposed to reflective) measurement. Separately, combining TCI and DAI into a single composite would violate the principle that constructs with different hypothesised nomological nets should remain analytically distinct.

**2.3 Hypothesis development**

***2.3.1 TCI direct effect on productivity***

Following the Lall (1992) capability tradition and the Cohen and Levinthal (1990) absorptive-capacity logic, firms with deeper technological capability should produce more output per unit of labour input regardless of internationalization stage. Technological capability reflects firm-internal investments in R&D, innovation, foreign technology licensing, and quality certification --- all of which raise the productivity floor by enhancing operational efficiency, product quality, and learning routines (Avenyo et al., 2021; Krammer et al., 2018). We therefore hypothesize:

> **Hypothesis 1 (H1).** *Technological capability (TCI) is positively associated with firm performance in Singapore.*

***2.3.2 TCI moderation of the I--P relationship***

A subtler prediction emerges from Rugman and Verbeke\'s (2004) non-location-bound firm-specific advantage (NLB-FSA) logic. Technological capabilities are firm-specific advantages that are recombinable across borders without significant adaptation cost. While TCI should raise the productivity intercept (H1), it should not interact with internationalization intensity to shift the I--P curvature, because the underlying capability depth is similarly available to the firm at all stages of export expansion.

> **Hypothesis 2 (H2).** *The TCI--productivity association in Singapore operates predominantly as a level-shift effect on the productivity intercept rather than as a curvature-shaping moderator of the internationalization--performance relationship.*

***2.3.3 DAI direct effect on productivity***

Digital adoption should generate productivity returns through reduced transaction costs, faster information flow, and broader market reach (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Verhoef et al., 2021). The magnitude of these returns, however, is unlikely to be uniform across firms. Tier 1--2 digital adoption (digital presence and electronic-transaction capability) primarily enables firms to scale existing operations through digital channels rather than to create capability depth de novo (Verhoef et al., 2021). The productivity payoff of such adoption should therefore depend on the extent to which the firm operates at scales where these scale-enabling features can be exercised intensively. At the digital frontier, where cross-border digital infrastructure is mature, the average DAI--productivity association is expected to be positive but modest at the unconditional mean, with most of the productivity premium materializing in firms whose operating scale --- in particular, their cross-border activity --- provides sufficient throughput for digital channels to be exploited efficiently. We therefore frame H3 as a directional but conditional claim, reserving the explicit moderation prediction for H4.

> **Hypothesis 3 (H3).** *Digital adoption (DAI) is positively associated with firm performance in Singapore on average, with the magnitude of this association expected to vary systematically with the firm's internationalization intensity (formally tested in H4).*

***2.3.4 DAI as a conditional complement to export intensity***

The most theoretically novel prediction concerns how digital adoption interacts with export intensity in a digitally mature institutional environment. In such settings, digital infrastructure may no longer generate a uniform productivity premium across all firms because basic digital functionality is already widely available. Its value may instead become more contingent on the scale and complexity of cross-border operations. Firms with high export intensity face greater coordination, transaction, and information-processing demands across markets, making digital adoption more valuable as a scale-enabling complement (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). By contrast, firms with low export intensity may realize weaker productivity benefits because they have fewer opportunities to amortize digital investments across international operations. The relationship is expected to be nonlinear, with digital adoption\'s productivity returns concentrated at the upper end of internationalization rather than uniformly distributed across export intensity. The marginal productivity of digital adoption is thus contingent on the extent to which cross-border complexity makes digital systems economically consequential. We therefore hypothesize:

> **Hypothesis 4 (H4).** *The association between digital adoption (DAI) and firm performance becomes more positive at higher levels of export intensity in Singapore.*

**2.4 Conceptual model**

Figure 1 summarizes the conceptual model. The model represents TCI and DAI as analytically distinct firm-level constructs, each with a directional association with firm performance (H1 and H3). TCI is theorized to operate as a level-shift effect on the productivity intercept rather than as a curvature-shaping moderator (H2), whereas DAI is theorized to interact with export intensity such that its productivity association strengthens at higher levels of FSTS (H4). H3 and H4 are jointly informative: H3 specifies the average directional association, and H4 specifies the contingency that governs its magnitude. The whole model is positioned within a digital-frontier institutional environment as the relevant boundary condition.

**3 Methods**

**3.1 Data**

We use the World Bank Enterprise Survey (WBES) Singapore 2023 wave, the most recent firm-level microdata available for Singapore. The WBES 2023 wave was administered under the B-READY methodology, which expanded the digital-adoption module to include items on electronic-payment penetration. After listwise deletion on focal variables, the analytic sample is N = 623 firms across all major sectors. Manufacturing accounts for approximately 31% of the sample, retail and other services for 50%, and other sectors for 19%. Eighteen percent of firms have non-zero exports (FSTS \> 0), and only 3% have FSTS exceeding 50%. Descriptive statistics and pairwise correlations are reported in Table 1.

**3.2 Variables**

***3.2.1 Dependent variable***

Consistent with the IB productivity-as-performance tradition (Avenyo et al., 2021; Krammer et al., 2018), we operationalize firm performance as labour productivity. Labour productivity is measured as ln(annual sales / full-time employees), constructed from WBES items d2 (annual sales) and l1 (full-time employees), following Krammer et al. (2018). All firms in this single cross-section share the 2023 fiscal reference year; sales figures are therefore expressed in nominal 2023 Singapore dollars, with the intercept absorbing any uniform price-level scaling. The variable is winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce sensitivity to extreme values.

***3.2.2 Focal independent variable***

Foreign-sales-to-total-sales (FSTS) is the proportion of annual sales from direct exports, derived from WBES item d3c and rescaled to the \[0, 1\] interval. FSTS is mean-centered before squaring (Aiken & West, 1991) to mitigate multicollinearity with the linear term. Variance inflation factors after centering remain below 3 in all specifications.

***3.2.3 TCI and DAI composites***

TCI and DAI are specified as formative composites rather than reflective scales because their indicators jointly constitute the construct and are not interchangeable manifestations of a single latent trait (Coltman et al., 2008). For TCI, the component items capture distinct dimensions of technological capability in the Lall (1992) tradition, including external technology linkage (WBES item e6, foreign-licensed technology), innovation output (h1, product innovation in the last three years), absorptive-capacity-building effort (h8, R&D spending), and operational mastery via quality certification (b8, ISO/quality certification). For DAI, the component items capture the firm\'s degree of participation in digital infrastructure and digital transactions, including website or digital presence (c22b), percentage of sales received via electronic payment (k33), and percentage of supplier payments via electronic payment (k38). Consistent with the Verhoef et al. (2021) hierarchy, these indicators capture Tier 1--2 digitalization only: website presence reflects basic digital presence, while customer- and supplier-side electronic payments reflect transactional digital use. They do not capture deeper digitally integrated organizational systems such as ERP, CRM, digitally integrated supply-chain management, or higher-order digital dynamic capabilities.

The label Digital Adoption Index is preferred over broader alternatives such as digital capability because the indicators capture firms\' adoption of digital interfaces and transaction-enabling mechanisms rather than deeper digitally integrated organizational capabilities (Verhoef et al., 2021); this naming choice reflects a measurement-validity decision that ensures the construct label matches the empirical content of the WBES indicators. Following the formative measurement logic of Coltman et al. (2008), the indicators are standardized, aggregated into equal-weighted composites, and then re-standardized for interpretation in standard-deviation units, consistent with the construction approach of Avenyo et al. (2021) and Krammer et al. (2018). Equal weighting is substantively appropriate in this context because the component items are conceptually co-equal manifestations of distinct sub-dimensions of each construct rather than redundant indicators of a single dominant facet (Coltman et al., 2008): for TCI, the four items capture conceptually distinct capability dimensions (external technology linkage, innovation output, absorptive-capacity-building effort, and operational mastery via certification) that the Lall (1992) tradition treats as analytically separable, while for DAI the three items capture conceptually distinct digital-interface dimensions (digital presence, customer-side digital transactions, and supplier-side digital transactions). Data-driven weighting schemes (e.g., principal-components weights) would conflate empirical variance shares with conceptual importance and risk mechanically downweighting items that are theoretically central but distributionally rare in the WBES sample. The R2 robustness specification (TCI_thin) and the R1 robustness specification (DAI_thin, single-indicator website) jointly verify that the central inferences are not driven by any single dominant component of either composite. In particular, the website-only restriction in R1 provides a deliberately thin Tier 1 version of DAI against which the richer baseline composite can be evaluated; the baseline specification is therefore preferable not because it measures digital capability in a broad sense, but because it more fully captures the Tier 1--2 digital-adoption domain observable in WBES. All coefficients on TCI and DAI are interpreted per one-standard-deviation increase in the composite.

***3.2.4 Controls***

We control for firm size (ln_empl, the natural logarithm of the number of full-time employees), firm age (year of survey minus year of establishment from WBES item b5), foreign-ownership status (binary, derived from WBES item b2b indicating ≥ 10% foreign equity), and broad sector fixed effects derived from the WBES sector classification (a4a). Sector indicators distinguish manufacturing, retail, and other services and absorb sector-level heterogeneity in average labour productivity, export propensity, technology-adoption norms, and digital-infrastructure penetration that would otherwise be loaded onto the focal regressors (cf. Avenyo et al., 2021; Krammer et al., 2018). Manager experience (WBES item b6), originally considered as a control, was dropped after a variance inflation factor diagnostic revealed severe collinearity with ln_empl (VIF = 6.11 before drop, 1.13 after; conventional threshold for moderate concern is 5; O\'Brien, 2007).

**3.3 Estimation strategy and identification scope**

We estimate ordinary-least-squares regressions with Sector FE = broad WBES sector dummies (manufacturing, retail, other services). HC1 robust standard errors throughout (MacKinnon & White, 1985). Models are nested progressively from a controls-only specification (M0) through an inverted-U baseline (M2), TCI direct effect (M5), DAI direct effect (M6), TCI + DAI joint direct effects (M7), DAI moderation (M4), and the full specification (M8). The equation below corresponds to the full model (M8); a supplementary TCI-moderation specification (M3), discussed in Section 4.3 but omitted from Table 2 for parsimony, replaces the DAI interaction block in this equation with the analogous TCI interaction terms (FSTS × TCI, FSTS² × TCI):

*ln(LP) = α + β₁·FSTS_c + β₂·FSTS_c² + β₃·TCI + β₄·DAI + β₅·(FSTS_c × DAI) + β₆·(FSTS_c² × DAI) + γ·controls + ε,*

where FSTS_c is mean-centered FSTS, FSTS_c² its square, and TCI and DAI are z-scored composites. To assess the inverted-U formally, we apply the Lind and Mehlum (2010) U-test. To assess the joint significance of DAI moderation, we apply an F-test of β₅ = β₆ = 0. We report Cohen\'s f² as the effect-size measure, following the conventions of Aguinis et al. (2005) for moderation effects.

Our identification scope is explicitly associational rather than causal, and we adopt the disciplined causal-language conventions advocated for international business and strategy research throughout the manuscript (Antonakis et al., 2010; Meyer et al., 2017; Shaver, 2020). The cross-sectional WBES Singapore 2023 design cannot rule out reverse causation or omitted-variable bias, and standard ex post diagnostics --- including non-significant inverse Mills' ratios from selection-correction models --- do not constitute affirmative evidence of unbiasedness in the absence of a valid exclusion restriction (Wolfolds & Siegel, 2019). Two selection mechanisms are particularly salient in our setting. First, productivity-driven self-selection into digital adoption: more productive firms possess the financial slack and absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) to install electronic-payment systems and digital interfaces, so the observed DAI--productivity association may reflect this selection channel rather than a productivity-enhancing effect of digital adoption itself. Second, productivity-driven self-selection into export markets, whereby only firms above a productivity threshold profitably overcome the fixed costs of exporting; the observed FSTS × DAI interaction could therefore reflect productivity-induced co-movement of export intensity and digital-system utilization rather than a digital-system-induced productivity gain. We therefore use the language of "association," "is consistent with," and "supports" rather than causal verbs throughout, restrict our interpretive scope to firm-level patterns observed in this single cross-section, and treat the moderation evidence as identifying a boundary condition rather than as a tested causal mechanism. Section 6 specifies the panel-data and quasi-experimental designs that would be required to upgrade these associational claims to a causal identification.

**4.2 The I--P relationship: primarily monotonic with mild curvature**

In the M2 inverted-U baseline specification (Table 2), the linear FSTS term is strongly positive (β = +3.464, SE = 0.902, p \< .001) and the quadratic FSTS² term is marginally negative (β = −2.059, SE = 1.082, p = .057). Because FSTS is mean-centered before squaring (sample mean ≈ 0.045, i.e., 4.5%), the formula −β₁/(2β₂) returns the implied turning point measured as a deviation from the sample mean (≈ 84%); converted to the original uncentered scale, this corresponds to FSTS ≈ 88.6%. Either way, the implied turning point lies in the sparsely populated right tail of the empirical FSTS distribution (only 3% of the sample exceeds 50% FSTS, and the 99th percentile is approximately 90%). The Lind and Mehlum (2010) joint U-test fails to reject the null of monotonicity (p = .301). Because the curvature is identified primarily from a sparsely populated right tail, we treat the apparent quadratic turning point as a descriptive feature of the within-sample fit rather than as a structurally identified inflection of the I--P relationship. Following Haans et al. (2016), we therefore characterize the I--P relationship as primarily monotonic positive with mild quadratic curvature rather than as a confirmed inverted-U, and we treat the curvature evidence as suggestive rather than as structurally identified. This finding qualifies the modal inverted-U pattern documented in earlier I--P literature (Marano et al., 2016) within the specific context of a digital-frontier economy: when cross-border coordination costs have been substantially attenuated by mature digital infrastructure, any right-side decline of the I--P curve is flattened or shifted to levels of export intensity that are sparsely represented in the observed sample.

**4.3 TCI direct effect supports H1; TCI moderation null supports H2**

In Model M5 (Table 2), TCI exerts a positive and strongly significant direct effect on labour productivity (β_z = 0.202, SE = 0.042, p \< .001), supporting H1. R² increases from .162 in M2 to .191 in M5. In the supplementary TCI-moderation specification (M3, not tabulated in Table 2), the TCI direct effect remains significant (β_z = 0.191, p \< .001), and the TCI moderation terms (FSTS × TCI and FSTS² × TCI) are jointly insignificant; a joint Wald test yields F(2, 612) = 0.48, p = .621. The pattern is consistent with H2's framing of TCI as a predominantly level-shift effect: the TCI direct coefficient and incremental R² in M3 (relative to M5) are several times larger than the contributions of the curvature-interaction terms, indicating that the bulk of the empirical action operates at the intercept rather than at the curvature. Because absence of moderation cannot be inferred from non-significance alone (Lakens et al., 2018), we frame this evidence as supporting an intercept-dominant interpretation of the TCI effect rather than as a definitive demonstration that curvature moderation is exactly zero. This pattern is consistent with the Rugman and Verbeke (2004) NLB-FSA recombinability prediction.

**4.4 DAI moderation: evidence concentrated in the high-export tail**

DAI exerts a significant direct effect on labour productivity in Model M6 (β_z = 0.117, SE = 0.037, p = .002), supporting the directional component of H3 and indicating that a one-standard-deviation increase in digital adoption is associated with a 0.117 increase in ln(labour productivity), approximately equivalent to an 11.7% higher labour productivity. When both TCI and DAI are included as direct effects in Model M7, the DAI coefficient attenuates but remains significant at the 5% level (β_z = 0.076, p = .049), suggesting partial overlap in the explanatory space captured by the two constructs. The attenuation is consistent with the conditional component of H3: a sizeable share of the unconditional DAI--productivity association reflects average-firm productivity heterogeneity that is also captured by TCI, with the genuinely DAI-specific premium expected to materialize primarily at higher levels of internationalization (formally tested in H4 below).

**4.5 Robustness checks**

Robustness checks reported in Table 3 indicate that the paper\'s central inference is broadly stable across alternative specifications, although the strength of the evidence varies with measurement choice and sample restriction. Under the baseline specification, the quadratic moderation term remains positive and significant, supporting the interpretation that digital adoption becomes more economically consequential at higher levels of export intensity rather than generating a uniform productivity premium across all firms. Throughout this section, the robustness evidence should be read within the measurement boundary of the DAI construct: the baseline index captures foundational digital presence and transaction usage rather than deeper digitally integrated organizational capability. The substantive stability of the moderation result therefore supports the manuscript\'s central claim about digital adoption at the Tier 1--2 level rather than a broader claim about digital capability in the stronger Tier 3--4 sense. The R1 specification restricts DAI to a single-indicator website-presence measure (c22b only) to verify that the moderation pattern is not driven by the two electronic-payment indicators (k33 and k38) included in the baseline DAI composite. Under this single-indicator restriction, the quadratic interaction loses individual significance (β = 1.563, ns), consistent with the loss of identifying variation that occurs when the richer DAI composite is reduced to website presence alone. This attenuation suggests that the baseline interaction draws meaningful variation from the electronic-payment indicators in addition to website presence.

Additional robustness specifications reported in Table 3 further support this interpretation by showing that the positive quadratic moderation pattern remains visible when technological capability is measured more parsimoniously and when the sample is restricted to larger or smaller firm segments. Collectively, these six specifications serve as a sensitivity probe for whether the OLS estimates are driven by any single operational decision --- the breadth of DAI, the breadth of TCI, the inclusion of micro-firms, an SME-only orientation, or exporter selection --- and the persistence of the FSTS² × DAI sign across all six rows constitutes the strongest internal-stability check available within the cross-sectional WBES design. For the exporters-only subsample (R5; N = 84), however, the estimates are appreciably less stable: the adjusted R² falls to 0.140, the joint F is significant only at the .033 level, and the right tail of FSTS is sparsely populated. We therefore treat the high-export-intensity inferences drawn from the exporters-only subsample as suggestive rather than definitive, and rely on the full-sample specifications (M8 and R3--R4) for the central inferential claim. Taken together, these results suggest that the manuscript's strongest empirical claim concerns the contingent, export-intensity-dependent role of digital adoption, whereas evidence for a uniform direct productivity premium is more specification-sensitive. Cohen's f² for the TCI direct effect (M2 → M5) is approximately 0.036, in the small-to-medium range; Cohen's f² for the DAI moderation block (M7 → M8) is approximately 0.018, below Cohen's (1988) conventional "small" threshold of 0.02 but consistent with typical magnitudes of moderation effects in organizational research (Aguinis et al., 2005).

**5 Discussion**

**5.1 Theoretical implications**

Three theoretical implications follow from these findings. First, the TCI result supports a capability-depth interpretation of firm performance in internationalizing firms. Technological capability appears to operate as a non-location-bound firm-specific advantage (Rugman & Verbeke, 2004) that raises the productivity intercept but does not systematically reshape the curvature of the I--P relationship. This pattern is consistent with the view that internal capability depth can improve how efficiently firms operate across markets without necessarily altering the coordination-cost dynamics that generate nonlinearity in the I--P relationship.

Second, the present evidence qualifies rather than overturns the conventional nonlinear I--P literature (Contractor et al., 2003; Hitt et al., 1997; Lu & Beamish, 2004; Marano et al., 2016). The baseline relationship remains mildly curved, but the negative right-hand side of the curve is not formally established within the observed export-intensity range. A useful interpretation is that, in a digital-frontier institutional environment, cross-border coordination costs do not disappear; instead, they are partially reallocated. Mature digital infrastructure reduces basic communication, transaction, and information frictions, which can flatten the right-side decline of the curve within the export-intensity range most firms actually occupy. Under such conditions, the classic inverted-U may still exist in principle, but its turning point is shifted toward the extreme upper tail of internationalization, where empirical observations are sparse in the present sample. This reading positions the contribution as a boundary-condition refinement rather than a competing claim: the I--P curvature documented in earlier samples drawn primarily from pre-digital and transitional-digital economies (Marano et al., 2016) need not generalize to digital-frontier institutional environments without explicit specification of the digital-infrastructure boundary under which it holds.

Third, the DAI result suggests that digital adoption in a digitally mature economy is best understood as a conditional scaling complement rather than as a universal productivity premium. When basic digital infrastructure is already widespread, digital adoption is less likely to differentiate firms uniformly across the full export-intensity distribution. Its productivity relevance becomes stronger when firms reach high export intensity, because digital interfaces and transaction systems can then be amortized across a larger volume of cross-border exchanges, denser information flows, and more demanding multi-market coordination tasks (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). This interpretation also clarifies why the construct should be labeled digital adoption rather than digital capability. The available WBES indicators capture firms\' uptake of digital interfaces and transaction-enabling mechanisms, not deeper digitally integrated organizational capabilities (Verhoef et al., 2021). Distinguishing DAI from TCI therefore does more than improve terminology; it clarifies that firm-internal capability depth and digitally enabled cross-border orchestration are analytically distinct sources of performance heterogeneity. In the present setting, the observed DAI effect should be understood as evidence about the productivity relevance of Tier 1--2 digitalization in a digital-frontier economy, not as direct evidence about deeper digitally integrated organizational capability.

**5.2 Managerial implications**

For firms operating in digital-frontier settings, the findings suggest that investments in digital adoption are most strongly associated with productivity advantages when firms already operate at high export intensity. In such cases, digital interfaces, payment systems, and related transaction-enabling tools appear to function as scaling mechanisms that help firms coordinate a larger volume of cross-border activity more efficiently.

For firms concentrated in domestic markets or at low export intensity, digital adoption may still be beneficial, but the productivity differentials appear smaller and less distinctive. In a digitally mature economy, basic digital functionality is already widespread, so adopting such tools may no longer generate a large standalone productivity premium for all firms equally.

For firms in the intermediate export-intensity range, the results suggest caution in expecting immediate labour-productivity gains from digital adoption alone. The evidence does not indicate a strong penalty in this range, but neither does it indicate reliably large gains. Managerially, this implies that digital adoption in this segment should be aligned with broader strategic objectives such as market access, customer integration, supplier coordination, and future export scaling rather than justified only on the basis of short-run productivity returns.

More broadly, the findings imply that managers should avoid treating technological capability and digital adoption as interchangeable investment categories. Investments in technological capability appear to support a broader productivity base, whereas investments in digital adoption seem to become especially valuable when firms face the coordination intensity associated with deeper internationalization. For managerial decision making, this means that capability-building and digitalization strategies should be sequenced and matched to the firm\'s actual stage of export expansion rather than pursued as a single undifferentiated \"digital capability\" agenda.

**6 Limitations and Future Research**

Three limitations warrant explicit caution in interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is cross-sectional, and the identification strategy is therefore associational rather than causal (Antonakis et al., 2010; Shaver, 2020). The observed positive associations between TCI, DAI, internationalization, and labour productivity admit at least two non-causal interpretations consistent with the data. The first is productivity-driven self-selection into digital adoption: more productive firms have the financial slack and managerial bandwidth to install electronic-payment systems and digital interfaces, so the observed DAI--productivity association may reflect this selection channel rather than a productivity-enhancing effect of digital adoption per se. The second is productivity-driven self-selection into international markets, in which only firms above a productivity threshold profitably overcome the fixed costs of exporting; the observed FSTS × DAI interaction could therefore reflect productivity-induced co-movement of export intensity and digital-system utilization rather than a digital-system-induced productivity gain. Standard ex post diagnostics, such as non-significant inverse Mills' ratios from Heckman-style models, do not constitute affirmative evidence of unbiasedness in the absence of a valid exclusion restriction (Wolfolds & Siegel, 2019). The disciplined response is therefore to restrict interpretation to a boundary-condition claim --- that the I--P curvature and the marginal value of digital adoption appear systematically conditional on export intensity in a digital-frontier setting (Meyer et al., 2017) --- while leaving causal identification to designs we cannot implement here.

Second, the present sample contains a thin right tail of high-intensity exporters, with only 18% of firms reporting non-zero exports and only 3% exceeding 50% export intensity. This constrains statistical precision exactly where the moderation effect is theoretically most relevant and suggests caution in generalizing from the upper end of the export distribution. Two specific consequences follow. First, the apparent quadratic turning point of the I--P curve at FSTS ≈ 88.6% (on the original uncentered scale) is identified primarily from a sparsely populated region of the FSTS distribution and should therefore be treated as a within-sample descriptive feature rather than as a structurally identified inflection. Second, the DAI moderation pattern in Table 4 attains conventional significance only at FSTS = 70% and FSTS = 100%, and the exporters-only subsample (R5 in Table 3, N = 84) yields a substantially lower adjusted R² than the full-sample specifications. The scale-enabling-complement finding is therefore best characterized as suggestive evidence consistent with a digital-frontier boundary condition rather than as a structurally identified universal pattern. Replication on larger samples of high-intensity exporters from comparable digital-frontier economies --- where the right tail of FSTS is denser and support-aware diagnostics can be applied more powerfully --- would substantially strengthen the inference.

Third, the estimated moderation effect is statistically detectable but substantively modest (Cohen's f² ≈ 0.018, below Cohen's "small" threshold although consistent with typical moderation effects in organizational research; Aguinis et al., 2005). The findings should therefore be treated as an initial firm-level test of a digital-frontier argument rather than as definitive evidence of a generalized boundary condition. The DAI indicators available in WBES 2023 capture Tier 1--2 digital adoption (digital presence and digital transaction usage) rather than Tier 3--4 digital capability or digital dynamic capability in the Verhoef et al. (2021) hierarchy; whether the scale-enabling-complement pattern persists, attenuates, or is amplified under richer measures of digitally integrated organizational capabilities is therefore an open empirical question. Future research should examine whether the same pattern appears in other digitally mature economies (e.g., Hong Kong, Republic of Korea, Taiwan) and under purpose-designed instruments that capture deeper digital dynamic capabilities (Verhoef et al., 2021). Future research should extend this measurement architecture by linking WBES microdata, where confidentiality arrangements permit, to proprietary firm-level databases such as Bureau van Dijk\'s Orbis, or by using purpose-built survey instruments modeled on the World Bank\'s Firm-level Adoption of Technology framework (Cirera, Comin, & Cruz, 2022), which can better capture process-level digital integration and higher-order capability depth.

Future research should also extend the two-construct architecture across digital-frontier economies, develop measurement instruments for finer-grained dimensions of digital adoption beyond basic website and electronic-payment indicators, and examine whether the scale-enabling-complement pattern operates through identifiable mechanisms (e.g., supplier-side digital integration, customer-facing digital channels, internal ERP integration; Banalieva & Dhanaraj, 2019; Stallkamp & Schotter, 2021). Causal identification, in particular, would benefit from firm-level panel data --- successive WBES waves, national statistical-office firm registers, or proprietary firm-level databases --- that permit firm fixed-effects estimation, which partials out time-invariant unobserved firm heterogeneity such as founder ability and intangible-capital stocks. Where suitable instruments are available (e.g., regional digital-infrastructure rollouts or plausibly exogenous trade-policy shocks), instrumental-variable or difference-in-differences designs would further upgrade the present associational evidence to a causal identification (Antonakis et al., 2010; Shaver, 2020). These designs would directly test whether the boundary condition documented here --- namely, that digital adoption operates as a scale-enabling complement at high export intensity in digital-frontier settings --- is causally generated rather than reflecting selection on unobserved firm-level performance heterogeneity.
